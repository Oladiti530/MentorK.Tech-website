Where Do I Start?
Welcome

A Word from MentorK.Tech

If you have ever typed "how do I get into tech" into a search bar and closed the tab feeling more confused than when you opened it, this book was written for you.

Every week, people message us with the same question in different words. "I want to learn tech but I don't know where to start." "Should I do coding or design?" "Is data science the same as data analysis?" "Everyone is talking about AI — do I need maths?" "I've watched fifty videos and still haven't started anything."

The problem is rarely a lack of information. The real problem is that nobody hands beginners a map. Without a map, every path looks equally scary, and the easiest choice becomes no choice at all.

This book is that map. It walks you through the major fields in technology one by one, in plain English, and tells you honestly what people in each field do all day, which tools they open in the morning, which programming languages they speak, and how a complete beginner takes the first steps. At the end, you will compare the fields side by side, answer a short self-check, and leave with a 30-day starting plan.

At MentorK.Tech we train, we mentor, and we build. We have watched people who "were not computer people" become confident developers, designers, analysts, and network engineers. The difference was never talent. It was a clear starting point and someone to walk alongside them. Consider this book the starting point. We would be glad to be the ones walking alongside you.

Part One

Before You Choose

Two things stop most beginners before they begin: believing they are not "the type", and not being able to see the whole picture. Let's fix both.

Chapter 1

You Belong in Tech

Before we talk about fields and tools, we need to deal with the voice in your head that says tech is for other people.

Almost everyone we have mentored arrived carrying at least one of the myths below. None of them survived the first month of real learning. Read them, recognise yours, and let it go.

Myth 1: "I need to be a maths genius."

Most tech jobs use far less maths than people fear. A front-end developer, a UI/UX designer, a network engineer, or a cybersecurity analyst can go years without solving an equation. Only a few specialised areas, like machine learning research, lean heavily on mathematics — and even there, you learn it as you go, with tools doing the heavy lifting. What you do need is logical thinking: the ability to break a problem into steps. That is a skill, and skills are learned.

Myth 2: "I'm too old / too young / started too late."

People switch into tech at 17 and at 47. Employers and clients care about what you can build, not when you were born. A 35-year-old accountant who learns data analysis actually has an advantage: years of understanding what the numbers mean.

Myth 3: "I need an expensive laptop."

For almost every field in this book, a basic laptop that can run a web browser and a text editor is enough to start. Many tools run entirely inside the browser. You can learn UI/UX design, web development, data analysis, and networking basics on a modest machine. Upgrade later, when you know what you need.

Myth 4: "I need a computer science degree."

A degree helps, but the tech industry is unusually open to people who can show their work. A portfolio of real projects, a GitHub profile, or a well-made app will get you past doors that a certificate alone will not. Many of the best people in the industry are self-taught or mentor-taught.

Myth 5: "I'm not a computer person."

Nobody is born a computer person. Everybody who is one today was once staring at a screen, confused. Tech is not a personality type; it is a collection of learnable skills, and different fields reward very different personalities. Some are for people who love visuals. Some are for people who love puzzles. Some are for people who love order and systems. Some are for people who love helping others. There is room for you.

Myth 6: "I have to pick the perfect field now."

You do not. Fields in tech overlap enormously. A front-end developer who learns databases becomes a full-stack developer. A data analyst who learns Python drifts into data science. A network engineer who studies attacks becomes a security professional. Your first choice is a starting point, not a life sentence. The goal of this book is to help you choose a good starting point quickly, so you can begin, and then adjust as you learn what you enjoy.

Myth 7: "I watched the videos, so I'm learning."

Watching is not learning. Doing is learning. If you have watched ten hours of tutorials and built nothing, you have not started yet. The good news: the moment you build something tiny — a one-page website, a spreadsheet dashboard, a small script — you will learn more than in all those hours of watching combined.

What you actually need
  • Curiosity — the willingness to ask "how does this work?"
  • Patience — things break; fixing them is the job, not a sign of failure.
  • Consistency — one hour every day beats ten hours once a month.
  • A starting point — which is what the rest of this book gives you.

A promise before you turn the page

Every chapter ahead will speak to you plainly. When a technical word appears, it will be explained. When a tool is named, you will be told what it is for. You are not expected to know anything yet. That is the whole point.

Chapter 2

The Map of Tech

"Tech" is not one job. It is a whole city with different neighbourhoods. Here is the city from above.

Think about what happens when you open a banking app on your phone and send money to a friend. In those few seconds, almost every field in this book was involved:

  • A UI/UX designer decided where the buttons go and how the screens flow.
  • A front-end / mobile developer built the screens you tap.
  • A back-end developer wrote the code that checks your balance and moves the money.
  • A cloud engineer keeps your account records safe and available 24/7.
  • A DevOps engineer shipped the app update without breaking anything.
  • A cybersecurity analyst is watching for fraud and attacks in the background.
  • A data scientist built the model that flagged whether this transfer looks suspicious.
  • A network engineer keeps your phone connected to the bank.
  • An AI engineer trained the chatbot you talk to when something goes wrong.

Every one of these people is "in tech", yet most would struggle to do each other's jobs. That is why "learn tech" is not a plan — but "learn front-end development" is.

The big idea

Tech fields are defined by what problem they solve, not by how hard they are. Pick the problem you find interesting, and the field follows.

The nine neighbourhoods

Software DevelopmentBuilds apps and programs for phones, desktops, and the web. "Make the thing work."
AI & Machine LearningTeaches computers to learn from data and make decisions. "Make the thing smart."
CybersecurityDefends systems and data from attackers. "Keep the thing safe."
Data ScienceTurns raw data into insights and predictions. "Make the numbers talk."
Cloud & DevOpsRuns and delivers software reliably at scale. "Keep the thing running."
UI/UX DesignPlans how products look and feel to use. "Make the thing usable."
Front-End DevelopmentCodes the screens people see and touch. "Build what the user sees."
Back-End DevelopmentCodes the logic, servers, and databases behind the screens. "Build what the user doesn't see."
Networking, Hardware & IT SupportKeeps computers, cables, and connections working. "Keep the lights on."
Where they meetFields overlap. Full-stack = front + back. MLOps = AI + DevOps. Your path can cross borders.

Software Development & Engineering

"Make the thing work."
In plain English
Software developers write the instructions that make computers, phones, and websites do useful things. Every app on your phone, every program on a laptop, every website you visit — someone wrote it, line by line. Software development is the craft of designing those instructions, writing them, testing them, and keeping them working as the world changes.
You'll enjoy it ifyou like building things and seeing them work
Main languagesPython, JavaScript/TypeScript, Java, C#, C++, Kotlin, Swift
Signature toolsVS Code, Git & GitHub, Docker, Jira
Time to first project2–4 weeks with daily practice
Chapter 3 · Software Development

What they actually do

A software developer's job is to translate a human need into working software. Someone says "our customers should be able to book an appointment from their phone", and the developer turns that sentence into an app that does exactly that — reliably, quickly, and without losing anyone's data.

A typical day looks less like a movie hacker scene and more like this:

  • Reading and planning. Understanding what needs to be built, and breaking it into small tasks.
  • Writing code. Adding new features, one small piece at a time.
  • Testing. Checking the code works — and, importantly, checking what happens when a user does something unexpected.
  • Fixing bugs. A bug is a mistake in the code. Finding and fixing them is a large part of the job and is genuinely satisfying once you get the hang of it.
  • Reviewing. Reading teammates' code and having yours read. This is how teams keep quality high and how juniors learn fastest.
  • Collaborating. Short meetings with designers, testers, and product managers to stay aligned.

Job titles you will see

TitleWhat it means
Software Engineer / DeveloperGeneral builder of applications. The most common title.
Mobile DeveloperBuilds apps for Android (Kotlin/Java) or iPhone (Swift), or both with Flutter or React Native.
Full-Stack DeveloperComfortable on both the user-facing side and the server side of a web app.
Desktop / Systems DeveloperBuilds programs that run on Windows, Mac, or Linux, often in C++, C#, or Java.
Game DeveloperBuilds games, usually with engines like Unity (C#) or Unreal (C++).
QA / Test EngineerSpecialises in testing software and writing automated tests. A common, friendly entry point.

Core tools, explained

Tools are just software that helps you make software. You will meet these four everywhere.

ToolWhat it is for
VS Code, IntelliJ IDEAAn IDE (Integrated Development Environment) — the program you write code in. Think of it as Microsoft Word for code: it colours your text, spots typos, and helps you run your program. VS Code is free and the best place for beginners to start.
Git & GitHubGit saves a history of every change to your code, so you can undo mistakes and work with others without overwriting each other. GitHub is the website where that history lives online — it is also where developers show off their work. Your GitHub profile becomes your portfolio.
DockerPackages your program with everything it needs so it runs identically on any computer. It ends the classic problem of "but it works on my machine!" You will not need this in your first months, but you will hear the word constantly.
Jira, TrelloProject boards where teams list tasks, assign them, and move them from "To do" to "Done". Not technical — just how teams stay organised.

The languages

A programming language is the set of words and rules you use to give instructions to a computer. Different languages are popular for different kinds of software:

LanguageBest known for
PythonEasiest to read. Great first language. Used everywhere from web back-ends to AI.
JavaScript / TypeScriptThe language of the web. Every website runs it. TypeScript is JavaScript with extra safety checks.
JavaLarge business systems, banking software, and Android apps. Very stable, very employable.
C#Windows applications, business software, and Unity games. Similar feel to Java.
C++When speed matters: game engines, operating systems, high-performance software. Harder; usually not a first language.
Kotlin / SwiftThe modern languages for Android apps (Kotlin) and iPhone apps (Swift).

Beginner advice: start with Python (if you want general programming) or JavaScript (if you want to build things you can see in a browser quickly). Your second language will take a quarter of the time the first one did.

Is this for you?

Tick the ones that sound like you. Four or more is a strong sign.

  • You enjoy building things and get a kick out of "it works!" moments.
  • You like solving puzzles and don't mind being stuck for a while before it clicks.
  • You are patient with small details — a missing comma can stop a whole program.
  • You like the idea of creating apps or tools that other people actually use.
  • You are happy to spend a lot of time reading, searching, and experimenting.
  • You prefer clear right-or-wrong feedback (the program runs, or it doesn't).

Beginner roadmap

Stage 1 Foundations (weeks 1–4)
  • Install VS Code and pick one language: Python or JavaScript.
  • Learn the basics: variables, conditions (if/else), loops, functions, lists.
  • Write tiny programs every day: a calculator, a guessing game, a to-do list in the terminal.
  • Create a GitHub account and upload your tiny programs. Yes, even the ugly ones.
Stage 2 Building real things (months 2–4)
  • Learn how programs read and save data (files, then a simple database).
  • Learn to use other people's code: libraries and packages.
  • Build one complete small project end-to-end: a budget tracker, a quiz app, a simple booking tool.
  • Learn Git properly: commits, branches, and pull requests.
Stage 3 Specialise and show your work (months 4–8)
  • Choose a direction: web, mobile, desktop, or games — and learn its main framework.
  • Build two or three portfolio projects that solve real problems for real people (a local business, a church, a school, a friend's shop).
  • Contribute a small fix to an open-source project on GitHub.
  • Start applying for internships, junior roles, or freelance jobs.
First project idea

A "Contact Me" web form for a real small business, that saves messages to a file or database. Small enough to finish, real enough to matter.

Artificial Intelligence & Machine Learning

"Make the thing smart."
In plain English
Normal software follows rules a human wrote: "if the password is wrong, show an error." Machine learning is different — instead of writing the rules, you show the computer thousands of examples and let it work out the rules itself. That is how your phone recognises your face, how streaming apps guess what you'll like, and how chatbots write sentences. AI engineers are the people who gather those examples, train the systems, and put them to work.
You'll enjoy it ifyou like patterns, experiments, and "what if" questions
Main languagesPython (the standard), R, C++, Julia
Signature toolsJupyter, TensorFlow, PyTorch, Hugging Face, Scikit-learn
Time to first project6–10 weeks (after Python basics)
Chapter 4 · AI & Machine Learning

What they actually do

An AI engineer's core loop is: collect data → prepare it → train a model → test it → deploy it → improve it. A model is simply the "learned" program that comes out of training; think of it as a very well-practised guesser.

In practice, the day is a mix of:

  • Working with data. Gathering it, cleaning it (real data is messy: missing values, typos, duplicates), and labelling it. This is often 60–70% of the job, and it is where careful people shine.
  • Training models. Choosing an approach, feeding data into it, and waiting — sometimes minutes, sometimes days.
  • Evaluating. Measuring how often the model is right, and — crucially — how it fails. A model that is 95% accurate but always wrong for one group of people is not a good model.
  • Deploying. Wrapping the model so an app or website can use it (this overlaps with back-end and DevOps skills).
  • Reading. The field moves so fast that keeping up is part of the work.

Job titles you will see

TitleWhat it means
Machine Learning EngineerBuilds and deploys models into real products. Strong programming + ML knowledge.
AI EngineerOften means building products on top of existing AI models (like large language models) using APIs. A very practical, fast-growing entry route.
Data ScientistOverlaps heavily; usually more analysis and less deployment. See Chapter 6.
ML ResearcherInvents new methods. Usually needs advanced study and heavy mathematics.
Prompt / AI Product SpecialistDesigns how people and businesses interact with AI tools. Lighter on code, heavy on clear thinking.
Honest note on maths

To use machine learning, you need comfortable basics: averages, percentages, simple graphs, and a willingness to learn a little statistics. To invent new machine learning, you need university-level maths. Almost all jobs are the first kind.

Core tools, explained

ToolWhat it is for
Jupyter NotebooksA workspace where you mix code, results, charts, and notes on one page — perfect for experimenting. Google Colab is a free version that runs in the browser on Google's computers, so you don't need a powerful laptop.
Scikit-learnA Python library with ready-made "classic" machine learning methods. The friendliest place to train your first model.
TensorFlow, PyTorchThe two big libraries for deep learning — the technique behind image recognition, speech, and chatbots. PyTorch is currently the more beginner-friendly of the two.
Hugging FaceA website and library where thousands of pre-trained models are shared for free. You can download a model that already understands language or images and adapt it, instead of training from scratch.
AI APIs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, etc.)Let you use powerful models over the internet with a few lines of code. This is how most "AI-powered" apps are built today.
pandas, NumPyPython libraries for handling tables of data and numbers. You will use them every single day.

The languages

LanguageWhy it matters here
PythonThe absolute standard. Nearly every AI tool is built for Python first. Learn this and nothing else at the start.
RPopular with statisticians and researchers. Useful, but optional.
C++Used inside the high-performance engines that power AI. Not needed until much later, if ever.
JuliaA newer language for fast scientific computing. Niche; good to know it exists.
SQLNot on most AI lists, but the language for pulling data out of databases — and you need data before you can train anything.

Beginner advice: do not start with AI. Start with Python. When you can write a program that reads a file and does something useful with it, you are ready for your first model.

Is this for you?

  • You are curious about why things happen and enjoy spotting patterns.
  • You like experiments: try, measure, adjust, try again.
  • You are comfortable with uncertainty — models are "usually right", never "always right".
  • You find it exciting, not scary, that the field changes every few months.
  • You don't mind spending hours cleaning messy data to get a good result.
  • You are willing to learn some statistics along the way.

Beginner roadmap

Stage 1 Python and data (weeks 1–6)
  • Learn Python basics (see Chapter 3, Stage 1).
  • Learn pandas: open a spreadsheet, filter rows, calculate averages, make a chart.
  • Get comfortable in Google Colab or Jupyter.
  • Learn the vocabulary: dataset, feature, label, training, testing, accuracy.
Stage 2 First models (months 2–4)
  • Use scikit-learn to train classic models: predict house prices, classify emails as spam or not.
  • Learn how to measure a model honestly (train/test split, accuracy, confusion matrix).
  • Use an AI API to build a small app — a chatbot for a business, a summariser, a quiz generator.
Stage 3 Deep learning and deployment (months 4–9)
  • Learn PyTorch basics and train an image classifier.
  • Use a pre-trained model from Hugging Face and fine-tune it for a local problem (Nigerian food recognition, local-language text, market-price prediction).
  • Put one model behind a simple web app so others can try it.
  • Write up your projects clearly — in AI, explaining your results is half the job.
First project idea

A WhatsApp-style FAQ bot for a small business, built on an AI API, that answers questions from a document you give it. Real, useful, and finishable in a weekend once you know Python.

Cybersecurity

"Keep the thing safe."
In plain English
Every system that stores something valuable — money, personal details, company secrets — is a target. Cybersecurity is the practice of building walls around those systems, watching for anyone trying to climb over, and responding fast when someone does. Security professionals think like attackers so they can defend like professionals. Some of them are even paid to break into systems — with permission — to find the holes before criminals do.
You'll enjoy it ifyou love investigating, and thinking "how would I get in?"
Main languagesPython, Bash, PowerShell, SQL, C/C++ (advanced)
Signature toolsWireshark, Kali Linux, Nmap, Metasploit, Burp Suite, Splunk
Time to first project4–8 weeks (networking basics first)
Chapter 5 · Cybersecurity

What they actually do

Security work splits into two broad sides, often called the blue team (defenders) and the red team (authorised attackers). Most beginners start blue.

  • Monitoring. Watching network traffic and system logs for anything unusual: a login at 3 a.m. from another country, a computer suddenly sending huge amounts of data out.
  • Investigating. When an alert fires, working out whether it is a real attack or a false alarm, and what the attacker touched.
  • Patching. Keeping software updated and closing known weaknesses (vulnerabilities) before they are exploited.
  • Penetration testing. With written permission, attempting to break into a company's systems the way a criminal would, then writing a report of what was found and how to fix it. This is "ethical hacking".
  • Setting up protections. Firewalls, encryption, access rules, two-factor authentication.
  • Educating people. Most breaches start with a human clicking a bad link. Training staff is real security work.

Job titles you will see

TitleWhat it means
Security Analyst / SOC AnalystMonitors and responds to alerts in a Security Operations Centre. The most common entry-level role.
Penetration TesterAuthorised attacker. Finds and reports weaknesses.
Security EngineerBuilds and maintains the protective systems.
Incident Responder / ForensicsInvestigates after a breach: what happened, how, and what was taken.
GRC AnalystGovernance, Risk and Compliance — policies, audits, standards. Less technical, very in demand.
An important line

The skills in this chapter are powerful. Using them on systems you do not own or have written permission to test is a crime, everywhere. Ethical hackers practise on purpose-built training platforms and labs. The whole profession is built on trust.

Core tools, explained

ToolWhat it is for
WiresharkCaptures and shows every packet of data passing through a network, like a microscope for internet traffic. You can literally watch a website request travel.
Kali LinuxA version of the Linux operating system that comes pre-loaded with hundreds of security tools. The standard toolbox for penetration testers.
NmapScans a network to discover which devices are connected and which "doors" (ports) they have open.
MetasploitA framework for testing whether a known vulnerability can actually be exploited on a system — used in authorised tests.
Burp SuiteSits between your browser and a website so you can inspect and modify what is being sent. The main tool for testing web application security.
Splunk (and other SIEMs)Collects logs from thousands of systems into one searchable place and raises alerts. This is the screen a SOC analyst watches.
FirewallsHardware or software that decides which traffic is allowed in and out of a network. The front gate.

The languages

LanguageWhy it matters here
PythonAutomating repetitive security tasks, writing scanners, parsing logs, building small tools.
Bash / ShellThe command line of Linux. Security people live in the terminal; this is non-negotiable.
PowerShellThe command line of Windows. Essential because most companies run Windows.
SQLUnderstanding databases lets you understand — and prevent — SQL injection, one of the most common attacks.
C / C++For advanced work like analysing malware or finding low-level bugs. Not a starting point.

Beginner advice: before any tool, learn how networks work (IP addresses, ports, DNS, HTTP) and get comfortable in a Linux terminal. Security is applied networking plus applied curiosity.

Is this for you?

  • You enjoy detective work — following clues to find out what really happened.
  • When you see a system, you naturally wonder how it could be tricked or broken.
  • You are careful, ethical, and comfortable with responsibility.
  • You can stay calm under pressure (incidents happen at bad times).
  • You like learning how things work under the hood — networks, operating systems.
  • You are happy to keep learning forever; attackers never stop, so neither do defenders.

Beginner roadmap

Stage 1 Foundations (weeks 1–6)
  • Learn networking basics: what an IP address is, what DNS does, how a web request travels.
  • Install Linux (or use a virtual machine) and learn the terminal: navigating, files, permissions, users.
  • Learn how operating systems, passwords, and encryption work at a basic level.
  • Start Python basics for scripting.
Stage 2 Hands-on labs (months 2–5)
  • Join a free legal practice platform (e.g. TryHackMe, Hack The Box, OverTheWire) and complete beginner paths.
  • Use Wireshark to inspect your own traffic; use Nmap on your own home network.
  • Learn the OWASP Top 10 — the ten most common web weaknesses — and test them on deliberately vulnerable practice apps.
  • Consider a foundation certificate (e.g. CompTIA Security+) as a structured study path.
Stage 3 Specialise (months 5–12)
  • Pick blue (SOC / defence) or red (penetration testing) and go deep.
  • Build a home lab: a few virtual machines you attack and defend.
  • Write up your lab exercises as reports — employers love seeing you can communicate findings.
  • Enter beginner-friendly Capture-the-Flag (CTF) competitions.
First project idea

A "security check-up" for a small business: review their Wi-Fi setup, password habits, software updates, and backups, then write a one-page plain-English report with fixes. No hacking required — and genuinely valuable.

Data Science

"Make the numbers talk."
In plain English
Every business collects data — sales, clicks, deliveries, complaints, sensor readings — usually far more than anyone can read. Data science is the skill of turning those mountains of numbers into clear answers: Which products are really making money? Why did customers leave last month? What will sales look like in December? Data scientists clean the data, ask it questions, find the patterns, and explain them in charts and words that decision-makers can act on.
You'll enjoy it ifyou like finding the story hidden in numbers
Main languagesPython, SQL, R (and Excel — seriously)
Signature toolsExcel, Power BI, Tableau, pandas, Jupyter, SQL Server, Spark
Time to first project2–3 weeks (with Excel or SQL)
Chapter 6 · Data Science

What they actually do

Data work is a ladder, and you can step on at any rung. At the bottom is data analysis — describing what happened. At the top is data science — predicting what will happen and recommending what to do. The daily work at all levels is:

  • Getting the data. Pulling it from databases, spreadsheets, websites, or apps, usually with SQL.
  • Cleaning it. Fixing dates written five different ways, removing duplicates, filling gaps. Unglamorous, essential, and where most of the time goes.
  • Exploring it. Making quick charts and summaries to see what is going on.
  • Analysing and modelling. Answering the actual question — from a simple comparison to a predictive model.
  • Communicating. Building dashboards, writing reports, and presenting to people who do not care about code but care a great deal about the answer.

Job titles you will see

TitleWhat it means
Data AnalystDescribes and visualises what has happened. Excel, SQL, Power BI/Tableau. The friendliest entry point in all of tech for non-programmers.
Business Intelligence (BI) AnalystBuilds dashboards and reports that a company runs on. Very close to data analyst.
Data ScientistAdds statistics and machine learning to predict and recommend. Python-heavy.
Data EngineerBuilds the pipelines that move and store data at scale. Overlaps with back-end and cloud.
Analytics EngineerSits between engineer and analyst: makes data clean and ready for everyone else.
Why MentorK.Tech recommends data analysis as a first step

Cloud, Systems & DevOps

"Keep the thing running."
In plain English
When you use an app, the code is not running on your phone alone — it is talking to powerful computers called servers somewhere in the world. "The cloud" simply means renting those servers from companies like Amazon, Microsoft, or Google instead of buying your own. Cloud and DevOps professionals set up those servers, keep them running 24/7, make them scale when a million people show up at once, and build the automated conveyor belts that carry new code from a developer's laptop to live users safely.
You'll enjoy it ifyou like systems, automation, and reliability
Main languagesBash, Python, Go, YAML/JSON (configuration)
Signature toolsAWS, Azure, GCP, Terraform, Docker, Kubernetes, GitHub Actions
Time to first project4–6 weeks (Linux basics first)
Chapter 7 · Cloud, Systems & DevOps

What they actually do

This chapter covers two closely related roles that share most of their tools.

Cloud Engineer / Architect

Designs and manages a company's computing footprint in the cloud. They decide which services to use, how to connect them securely, how to keep costs down, and how to make sure the system stays up. A common early project is migration: moving a company's old in-office servers into the cloud without anyone noticing.

DevOps Engineer

"DevOps" joins Development and Operations. Traditionally, developers wrote code and threw it over a wall to an operations team who tried to make it run. DevOps engineers remove the wall. They build pipelines — automated steps that test new code, package it, and release it, often many times a day — so that changes go live fast and safely.

Day to day, both roles involve:

  • Writing configuration and scripts rather than app features. Much of the "code" is describing infrastructure.
  • Monitoring dashboards: CPU, memory, errors, response times. Being on call when things break.
  • Automating anything done more than twice.
  • Security and cost reviews — a misconfigured cloud account can leak data or burn thousands of dollars in a night.

Job titles you will see

TitleWhat it means
Cloud EngineerBuilds and operates cloud infrastructure. Entry route via certifications.
Cloud ArchitectSenior; designs whole systems. Years of experience.
DevOps EngineerOwns pipelines, automation, and deployments.
Site Reliability Engineer (SRE)Focuses on uptime and performance; strong coding.
Systems AdministratorThe classic role: manages servers, users, and backups. Great foundation.

Core tools, explained

ToolWhat it is for
AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud (GCP)The three big cloud providers, each offering hundreds of rentable services: servers, storage, databases, AI. Learn one deeply (AWS is most common); the others will feel familiar.
LinuxThe operating system running most servers in the world. Not optional in this field.
DockerPackages an application and its needs into a container that runs the same everywhere. The building block of modern deployment.
KubernetesManages hundreds of containers: starts them, restarts crashed ones, spreads them across servers. Complex; learn after Docker.

Is this for you?

  • You like things to be orderly, reliable, and automated.
  • You enjoy understanding how systems fit together more than designing screens.
  • You are calm when something is on fire and can work through a checklist.
  • You find it satisfying to make a slow, manual process fast and automatic.
  • You are happy living in a terminal and reading documentation.
  • You are comfortable with responsibility — real users depend on your systems.

Beginner roadmap

Stage 2 Cloud and containers (months 2–5)
  • Open a free-tier account on AWS (or Azure/GCP). Launch a server, host a website on it, set up a database.

  • Learn Docker: containerise a simple app you (or a friend) built.
  • Build a GitHub Actions pipeline that tests and deploys that app automatically.
Stage 3 Infrastructure as code and scale (months 5–10)
  • Recreate your cloud setup with Terraform.
  • Learn Kubernetes basics and deploy a multi-container app.
  • Add monitoring and alerts. Break things on purpose and practise recovering.
  • Document everything in a public repository — your infrastructure projects are your portfolio.
First project idea

Take a friend's website or portfolio, put it on a cloud server with a proper domain and HTTPS, and set it up so that every change they push to GitHub goes live automatically. That is DevOps in miniature.

The Web Stack: UI/UX, Front-End & Back-End

"Design it. Build what they see. Build what they don't."
In plain English
Every website and web app is made of three layers built by three kinds of people. The UI/UX designer plans how it should look and feel, before any code. The front-end developer builds the part you see and click in your browser. The back-end developer builds the invisible engine behind it: the server, the logic, and the database that remembers everything. Together, they are the "web stack" — and this is the most popular starting point in tech, because you can see your results within days.
You'll enjoy it ifyou want to build things people can use immediately
Main languagesHTML, CSS, JavaScript/TypeScript; Python, Node.js, Java, Go, C#
Signature toolsFigma, VS Code, React, Next.js, Tailwind, Node.js, PostgreSQL, Postman

Chapter 8 · The Web Stack

Layer 1 — UI/UX Design

UI (User Interface) is how it looks: colours, layout, buttons, typography. UX (User Experience) is how it works for a person: can they find what they need, is the journey from "open app" to "done" smooth or frustrating? UI/UX designers research users, sketch flows, build clickable prototypes, and hand a finished design to developers.

What they do daily: interview or observe users; map out the screens and the path between them (a "user flow"); draw rough sketches (wireframes), then polished designs; test prototypes with real people; maintain a design system — the reusable buttons, colours, and components a product is built from.

ToolWhat it is for
FigmaThe industry standard. Design screens, build clickable prototypes, collaborate live. Free to start and runs in the browser.
Adobe XD, SketchAlternatives to Figma. Less common now, but you may meet them.
FigJam, MiroWhiteboards for user flows, brainstorming, and research notes.

Programming languages: none. UI/UX is a visual and structural discipline. Knowing a little HTML/CSS helps you talk to developers, but it is not required. This is the best field in this book for creative people who do not want to code.

Is UI/UX for you?
  • You notice when an app is confusing and can say exactly why.
  • You care about how things look and how they work.
  • You like talking to people and understanding their frustrations.
  • You enjoy iterating: draft, feedback, improve, repeat.
Roadmap UI/UX

Weeks 1–4: learn design basics (layout, spacing, colour, type) and Figma. Redesign one screen of an app you use daily. Months 2–4: learn UX process — research, flows, wireframes, prototypes, usability tests. Design a complete small app. Months 4–6: build three case studies (problem → process → solution) into a portfolio site. Design a real product for a real client, even for free.

Layer 2 — Front-End Development

Front-end developers take the designer's screens and turn them into a working website or app that runs in a browser. They make it look right on a phone and a laptop, make buttons do things, and connect the screens to the back-end so real data appears.

What they do daily: build pages and components from designs; make layouts responsive (they adapt to any screen size); handle user actions (clicks, forms, scrolling); fetch data from the back-end and display it; test in different browsers; care about speed and accessibility so everyone can use the site.

Language / ToolWhat it is for
HTMLThe structure: headings, paragraphs, images, links, forms. Your first language — learnable in days.
CSSThe style: colours, fonts, spacing, layout, animation. Makes HTML look like a design.
JavaScriptThe behaviour: what happens when you click, type, or scroll. The only programming language browsers understand.
TypeScriptJavaScript with type checking that catches mistakes early. Standard in professional teams; learn after JavaScript.
React, Next.jsA library (React) and a framework built on it (Next.js) for building complex, fast apps out of reusable components. The most in-demand front-end skills.
Tailwind CSSA popular way to write CSS quickly using ready-made utility classes.
VS Code, Git, browser DevToolsYour editor, your history, and the built-in inspector in Chrome/Firefox that lets you see and tweak any website live.
Is front-end for you?
  • You want to see results instantly — change code, refresh, see it.
  • You like the mix of visual work and logic.
  • You care about the details of how things feel to use.
Roadmap Front-End

Weeks 1–4: HTML and CSS. Build and publish a one-page personal site (free hosting on GitHub Pages, Netlify or Vercel). Months 2–4: JavaScript fundamentals; make pages interactive; fetch data from a public API. Months 4–7: React, then Next.js and TypeScript; build two or three real sites for real people; make them fast and mobile-friendly.

Layer 3 — Back-End Development

Back-end developers build the engine room. When you log in, search, pay, or upload a photo, the front-end sends a request to the back-end, which checks it, does the work, saves or fetches data from a database, and sends the answer back. Users never see the back-end, but nothing works without it.

What they do daily: design and build APIs (the "menu" of requests an app can make); design databases; handle logins and security; integrate payments, SMS, and email; make things fast under heavy use; write tests.

Language / ToolWhat it is for
Node.js (JavaScript)Runs JavaScript on the server, so one language covers front and back. Very popular for startups.
Python (Django, FastAPI, Flask)Readable and fast to build with. Great first back-end language.
Java, C#, Go, RubyJava/C# in large companies and banks; Go for high-performance services; Ruby (Rails) in many startups.
PostgreSQL, MySQLRelational databases: data in tables, queried with SQL. Learn one; PostgreSQL is an excellent choice.

  • You care more about how things work than how they look.
  • You like logic, structure, and data.
  • You enjoy thinking about edge cases: "what if two people pay at the same second?"
Roadmap Back-End

Weeks 1–4: one language (Python or JavaScript). Months 2–4: build a simple API; connect a database; learn SQL; test with Postman. Months 4–7: add logins, uploads, and payments; deploy to the cloud; build a back-end for a real front-end. You are now full-stack.

"Keep the lights on."

Chapter 9 · Networking & Hardware

What they actually do

Network Engineer / Technician

Designs, installs, and maintains the networks that connect computers — inside an office, across a campus, or between cities. Configures routers and switches, sets up Wi-Fi, assigns addresses, secures the network, and troubleshoots when connections fail. Larger organisations, banks, ISPs, and universities all employ them.

Hardware / Computer Engineer

Works with the physical machines: assembling and upgrading computers, diagnosing faulty components, repairing laptops and phones, setting up servers, and in more advanced roles designing electronics and embedded systems (the small computers inside cars, appliances, and devices).

IT Support Specialist / Helpdesk

The front line. Sets up new staff, fixes software and hardware problems, manages user accounts, installs updates, and keeps everything working. It is the classic first job in tech and a launchpad into networking, security, cloud, or systems administration.

Chapter 12

Your First 30 Days

Whatever field you chose, the first month looks the same: set up, learn the basics, build one tiny thing, and show it to someone.

This plan assumes about one hour a day. If you have more time, go faster; if less, stretch it to 60 days. The order matters more than the speed.

Week 1 — Set up and commit

  1. Choose your field using Chapter 11. Write it on paper and put it where you will see it.
  2. Install your tools. One editor (VS Code, Figma, Excel, or Packet Tracer). Create a free GitHub account regardless of field.
  3. Pick one beginner course — a single free, well-reviewed one. Not five. Not "the best one". One.
  4. Fix your schedule. Same time every day. Tell someone you're doing it.

Week 2 — Learn the basics by doing

  1. Follow the course — but type every example yourself. Never copy-paste.
  2. Break things on purpose. Change a value; see what happens. This is how understanding grows.
  3. Keep a daily log: what you learned, what confused you, one question to answer tomorrow.

Week 3 — Build one tiny thing

  1. Choose a project you can finish in a week. Use the "First project idea" from your chapter, or something smaller.
  2. Finish it, even if it's ugly. Done is the goal. Perfect is the enemy.
  3. Put it online — GitHub, a Figma link, a published dashboard, a diagram and photos.

Week 4 — Show it and plan the next step

  1. Show your project to three people. One who knows nothing about tech, one who knows a little, one who knows a lot. Listen to all three.
  2. Write a short post about what you built and what you learned. It doesn't matter how many people read it. Writing it is what matters.
  3. Find a community — a local meetup, an online group, or a mentorship programme like MentorK.Tech. Learning alone is the slowest way.
  4. Plan month two. Look at Stage 2 of your chapter's roadmap and pick the next tiny project.
The only rule

Do not restart. Beginners who fail usually don't fail at coding or design — they fail by switching courses, switching fields, or switching languages every time it gets hard. It will get hard around week two. That is normal. Keep going for the full thirty days before you judge anything.

What "progress" really looks like

New learners expect a smooth upward line. Real learning looks like this:

  • Days 1–5: Excitement. Everything is new and it feels easy.
  • Days 6–15: The dip. Concepts pile up, errors multiply, and you wonder if you're "cut out for it". Everyone feels this. Everyone.
  • Days 16–25: The first click. Something that was confusing suddenly makes sense. You start to fix your own errors.
  • Days 26–30: Momentum. You finish something. You want to build the next thing.

If you know the dip is coming, it loses its power. Push through it once, and you will never be a beginner in the same way again.

Thirty days from now

You will have: a chosen field, working tools, one finished project online, a log of what you learned, and a plan for month two. That is more than most people who "want to get into tech" ever achieve. Start today.

Chapter 13

Mistakes Beginners Make

We have mentored enough beginners to know exactly where they trip. Here are the traps, and how to step around them.

1. Tutorial hell

Watching course after course without building anything. It feels productive and produces nothing. Fix: the moment you learn a concept, use it in a project of your own, however small.

2. Tool-hopping

"Should I learn React or Vue? Python or JavaScript? AWS or Azure?" — asked for months while learning none of them. Fix: the first choice barely matters. Skills transfer. Pick the one in this book and move.

3. Learning in secret

Waiting until you are "good enough" before showing anyone. You never feel good enough, so you never share, so you never get feedback, so you never improve. Fix: share from week one. A GitHub with ugly beginner code is worth more than a perfect one that doesn't exist.

4. Copy-pasting instead of typing

Your fingers learn alongside your brain. Copy-paste teaches neither. Fix: type everything, even when it's slow.

5. Skipping the fundamentals

Jumping to React before JavaScript, to machine learning before Python, to Kali Linux before networking. It works until it very suddenly doesn't. Fix: follow Stage 1 of your roadmap even when it feels basic.

6. Comparing your day 10 to someone's year 5

Social media is full of people showing their finished work, never their struggle. Fix: compare yourself only to yourself last month.

7. Buying gear instead of building skill

A new laptop, a mechanical keyboard, three monitors — none of it makes you learn faster. Fix: use what you have until it genuinely blocks you. Then upgrade the one thing that's blocking you.

8. Fearing error messages

Beginners see red text and panic. Professionals see red text and read it — because the error message usually says exactly what's wrong and where. Fix: read the error slowly. Search the exact message online. Nine times out of ten, someone has hit it before you.

9. Not asking for help — or asking badly

Struggling alone for days is not heroic; it is slow. But "it doesn't work, please help" gets no answers. Fix: ask early, and ask well: what you're trying to do, what you tried, what happened, and the exact error. You will often solve it yourself while writing the question.

10. Giving up in the dip

Around week two or three, almost everyone hits a wall and concludes they're "not a tech person". This is the single biggest reason beginners quit — and it is the most predictable, most temporary stage of all. Fix: re-read Chapter 12. Expect the dip. Keep your daily hour. Get a mentor or a study partner who has been through it and can tell you it passes.

The pattern behind every mistake

Look at the list again. Almost every trap is a way of avoiding the discomfort of building something real. Watching, choosing, buying, hiding, waiting — all of them feel like progress and cost nothing. Building feels uncomfortable and costs effort. That is exactly why it works.

One sentence to remember

The person who builds one small, imperfect thing every week will, in a year, be unrecognisable — and will be far ahead of the person still choosing the perfect course.

FrameworkA ready-made structure for building a certain kind of app quickly (React for interfaces, Django for back-ends).
Front-endThe part of a website or app that runs in the user's browser or on their screen.
Full-stackA developer comfortable with both front-end and back-end work.
Git / GitHubGit tracks every change to your code; GitHub hosts it online and doubles as a portfolio.
IDEIntegrated Development Environment — the program you write code in, like VS Code.
IP addressThe unique number identifying a device on a network, like a postal address for computers.
Library / PackageCode someone else wrote that you can reuse, so you don't rebuild common things from scratch.
LinuxA free operating system that runs most servers and is the home of security and DevOps work.
Machine learning modelA program that has "learned" patterns from example data and can make predictions on new data.
Open sourceSoftware whose code is public and free to use, study, and improve. Contributing to it is a great way to learn.
Pipeline (CI/CD)An automated sequence that tests, builds, and releases code whenever it changes.
PortA numbered "door" on a device through which a particular kind of network traffic passes.
PortfolioA collection of your real projects that shows employers and clients what you can do. More powerful than any certificate.
PrototypeA rough, clickable version of a design used to test ideas before building.
Responsive designA website that adapts its layout to phones, tablets, and desktops.
ServerA computer whose job is to serve data or services to other computers, usually running 24/7.
SQLStructured Query Language — the standard language for asking databases questions.
Terminal / Command lineA text-based way of controlling a computer by typing commands. Essential in most technical fields.
UI / UXUser Interface (how it looks) and User Experience (how it feels to use).
Version controlTracking changes to files over time so you can go back, compare, and collaborate. Git is the standard.
Virtual machine (VM)A computer running inside another computer as software. Lets you try Linux or build a lab without extra hardware.
VulnerabilityA weakness in software or a system that an attacker could exploit.
WireframeA simple sketch of a screen's layout, before colours and details are added.
